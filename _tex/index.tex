\begin{abstract}
As damages from floods, fires, and other climate extremes mount, public
investments in risk reduction have also grown. In the US, public
spending on risk reduction efforts totaled over \$X in 2024, and demand
for such funds regularly exceeds available funding. Evidence suggests
that in this competitive landscape for funding, urban and whiter
communities have fared better at obtaining funding but are often
spending it on more disadvantaged neighborhoods within them. However,
the reasons for this trend are unclear: complexities in the application
process, cost-benefit analysis requirements, and flood damage may all
play a role in driving the uneven funding patterns. Here, using a novel
dataset tracking properties from flood exposure to application to
funding receipt, we identify how each hurdle in the mitigation process
shapes the properties that ultimately benefit. Our findings suggest that
application for federal flood mitigation assistance serves as a pinch
point where homeowners in less-white neighborhoods and more affordable
homes relative to their neighbors apply. This trend deepens for those
who ultimately receive funding, while higher flood-exposure has minimal
correlation, implying that funding may not reach all who need it or may
be interested.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Impacts from natural hazards in the United States are increasing in both
cost and frequency
\citep{noaanationalcentersforenvironmentalinformationUSBillionDollarWeather2024}.
As demand for climate resilience measures has grown significantly in
recent years, so too have widespread concerns about whether access to
those funds perpetuates or mitigates existing inequities
\citep{OpenFEMADatasetHazard2023, junodEquitableInvestmentsResilience2021a, millerCostCostEffectivenessExpanding2023}.
Evidence suggests that while vulnerable groups --- including mobile home
residents and racial minorities --- are over-represented in flood
hotspots, wealthier and whiter communities have received
disproportionately more federal funding to adapt to and recover from
floods
\citep{elliottRacialInequitiesFederal2020a, tateFloodExposureSocial2021}.
Several explanations for this disparity have been proposed, including
federal cost-benefit analysis requirements, limited local government
capacity to apply for grants, and lack of household awareness about
funding opportunities. However, research to pinpoint funding obstacles
has been constrained by only observing who ultimately receives funding,
not who is eligible or who applies. As a result, the specific barriers
remain unclear, and it is therefore uncertain which policy or procedural
changes would most improve access to climate resilience funds.

Here, we combine novel datasets spanning over two decades of flood
exposure and applications for federal funding in North Carolina to
examine the full process: who floods, who applies for mitigation
funding, and who receives it. This flooded-to-funded pipeline (Fig. 1)
is critical for understanding how households navigate the Hazard
Mitigation Assistance (HMA) process. By analyzing household- and
neighborhood-level characteristics at each stage, we provide new
evidence on how the application process shapes the distribution of
funding. For example, if households in marginalized communities rarely
submit applications, the primary issue may be lack of awareness or
limited resources for applying. In contrast, if they are applying but
not being funded, the selection criteria may be driving disproportionate
access. Flood exposure is measured using a novel random-forest-based
flood inundation reconstruction that identifies the probability of flood
damage at every 30 m grid cell in eastern North Carolina across 78
events from 1996 to 2020, validated against address-level National Flood
Insurance Program (NFIP) records
\citep{garciaReconstructingRepetitiveFlood2025}.

Our initial results demonstrate that the application stage is the
critical pinch point in the mitigation funding pipeline. Of all flooded
properties, 67.5\% are eligible for public assistance and 61\% are
located in communities where local governments have submitted
applications. However, only 2.3\% of all flooded properties apply and
approximately 1\% are ultimately funded. Properties that do apply are
significantly more likely to have experienced more than one flooding
event. Compared to the broader population of flooded properties, those
that apply have lower property values (declining from the 58th to the
42nd percentile statewide) and are located in census block groups with a
lower share of white residents (declining from 74\% to 64\%). These
patterns deepen further among properties that receive funding. While
consistent with some nationwide findings linking wealthier and whiter
communities to greater mitigation access
\citep{machManagedRetreatVoluntary2019, elliottRacialInequitiesFederal2020a},
our analysis adds resolution: applying and funded properties are also
lower in value relative to their immediate flooded neighbors, not just
to the state overall. Notably, these trends emerge at the newly visible
application stage and widen among properties ultimately funded. We are
the first to demonstrate that unpacking this stage is pivotal to
understanding potential obstacles to equitable mitigation funding.

\subsection{Access to federal funding for climate
resilience}\label{access-to-federal-funding-for-climate-resilience}

The federal government plays a central role in supporting disaster
recovery and risk mitigation in the United States, so ensuring that
federal assistance is reaching those who need it most is crucial. Across
the federal level, many entities are involved at different stages in the
disaster risk management cycle, including the US Army Corps of
Engineers, the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, and the
Environmental Protection Agency. However, we focus our discussion and
analysis on the Federal Emergency Management Agency, which is the lead
entity responsible for disaster response and also a primary funder of
risk reduction projects across the US.

There have been long-standing concerns about access to FEMA assistance
in the immediate aftermath of disasters. Once a presidential disaster
declaration is issued to authorize individual assistance, FEMA operates
several programs to provide assistance with immediate post-disaster
needs. While these programs are no replacement for insurance, they do
provide up to about \$44k for funds for temporary housing, repair or
replacement of personal property, unemployment assistance, and
transportation or accessibility needs. Racial and income disparities in
accessing this post-disaster government assistance have been documented
for decades, including the 1996 Northridge earthquake \citep{bolin1998},
Hurricane Katrina (xx), Hurricane Dolly in 2008 \citep{rivera2022}, and
the Thomas Fire in 2017 \citep{mendez2020}. Many reasons for these
disparities have been documented, including informational barriers,
language challenges, biased home inspections, and other hurdles in the
application process.

More recently, concerns have emerged about access to FEMA's risk
mitigation programs. These fund efforts to reduce long-term risk, rather
than recover in the immediate aftermath of an event. Typical projects
include home elevations, construction of tornado shelters, and property
buyouts. More recently, under the Building Resilient Infrastructure and
Communities Program (BRIC), FEMA has also funded larger, community-wide
risk mitigation projects.

Property-scale projects (e.g., home elevations, property buyouts) are
typically funded under the Hazard Mitigation Assistance Program (HMA),
which includes several programs within it: Flood Mitigation Assistance
(FMA), Pre-Disaster Mitigation (PDM), and the Hazard Mitigation Grant
Program (HMGP). These programs have their own specific eligibility and
selection criteria, but all provide funds for property-level flood
mitigation. {[}insert sentence on budget of HMA programs.{]}

A number of studies have raised concerns about the equity of home
elevations and property buyouts, in which a homeowner sells their home
to the government for it to be returned to open space. Nationwide
analyses find that more urban and whiter counties were more likely to
receive funding for buyouts, perhaps because of their capacity to apply
for and administer grants \citep{mach2019, elliott2020}. Within those
counties, however, communities of lower socioeconomic status and a
higher share of racial minorities were more likely to be the locations
of buyouts. Home elevation funding has often gone to communities with
median incomes over \$100k or are over 90\% white (Frank, 2022).

\subsection{Procedural challenges to accessing
funding}\label{procedural-challenges-to-accessing-funding}

While these studies have presented a number of reasons for funding
disparities, pinpointing the chief obstacles has been elusive because of
a lack of information on applications for funding, making it impossible
to see who is not being selected vs.~not being considered in the pool at
all. The process for obtaining HMA funding is complicated. As shown in
Figure~\ref{fig-funding-pipeline}, FEMA first releases the funding
opportunity. State and local governments then work on developing
potential projects by contacting eligible residents, collecting
documentation on the property, and conducting a cost-benefit analysis of
the project. Local governments (the ``sub-applicants'') submit project
information to the state (the ``applicant''). States may or may not
select a subset of projects to then submit to FEMA. FEMA reviews
projects, then notifies states and local governments which projects are
funded. The federal government typically covers 75\% of project costs,
with the remaining 25\% either falling to some combination of the
resident, local government, and state government.

Disparities in access to the funds can arise at many points in this
process. Some states provide more support in the project development
process than others. Some local governments may choose not to
participate at all, due to capacity or financial constraints -- thus
making it impossible for their flood-prone residents to access the
funds. Notably, North Carolina covers the 25\% non-federal match
required, making it more accessible for cash-strapped local governments.
Others local governments may be willing, but do little to no outreach to
residents, such that only highly aware and connected residents benefit.
The cost-benefit analysis, a mandatory part of project evaluation, may
also skew selection.

While most flooded properties are eligible and in communities that have
applied for funding, very few properties submit applications (Fig. 2).
Among all flooded properties (Fig. 2, left panel), we find that around
2/3 are eligible for federal funding (67.5\%). Of these eligible
